Issue 003 · October 28, 2026 · Microsoft Security

Your Microsoft 365 MFA is enabled. But is it phishing-resistant?

A five-point review to close the gap between MFA enrollment and effective protection before Microsoft's 2027 SMS and voice changes.

Seeing "MFA enabled" in an admin portal is reassuring. It is not the same as knowing which sign-ins are protected against phishing. Some users may still depend on text messages or voice calls. Administrators may use everyday accounts for privileged work. Conditional Access exceptions may be wider than intended. A configuration checkbox cannot answer those questions.

This matters because attackers increasingly target the authentication process, not just passwords. A stolen password plus a phishable second factor can still put business email, files, and administrative access at risk.

Why now

Microsoft began rolling out passkeys as a default authentication experience in September 2026. Its current Entra ID guidance says Microsoft-provided SMS and voice delivery is scheduled to retire on February 1, 2027 for most users, with July 1, 2027 applying to Global Administrators and external users. These dates apply to the Entra ID public cloud; confirm the applicable timeline for your tenant. This is a reason to plan, not a reason to abruptly disable working access.

Five checks worth making this week

1. Who still relies on SMS or voice?

Inventory authentication methods and actual sign-in usage. Separate users who merely have a method registered from those who depend on it. Include executives, finance users, contractors, and administrators. Assign an owner to each exception.

2. Does policy cover the sign-ins that matter?

Review whether Security Defaults or appropriately licensed Conditional Access policies protect users, applications, and privileged actions. Check exclusions and legacy authentication paths. Do not disable Security Defaults without equivalent replacement controls. Test policy changes before enforcing them broadly.

3. Are privileged identities handled differently?

Separate administrative identities from daily productivity accounts, minimize standing privilege, and prioritize phishing-resistant authentication for high-impact roles. Maintain and periodically test documented emergency-access procedures so a policy mistake does not lock out the tenant.

4. Can users enroll and recover safely?

Pilot passkeys or other supported phishing-resistant methods with a small group first. Confirm device compatibility, onboarding, account recovery, help-desk support, and workflows for shared or restricted devices. A technically sound control that blocks legitimate work without a recovery path will not last.

5. Can leadership see evidence, not assumptions?

Track users still dependent on weaker methods, policy coverage, high-risk exceptions, rollout progress, and the owner of each remediation item. Keep dated configuration evidence and review it on a defined cadence. A Secure Score alone is not proof of effective enforcement.

A 30-minute question for your MSP or IT team

Ask for four answers and one name: (1) how many active users still depend on SMS or voice, (2) how many privileged accounts lack phishing-resistant authentication, (3) how many Conditional Access or authentication-policy exceptions exist, (4) when emergency access was last tested, and (5) who owns the migration plan. You do not need a new product purchase to start this conversation.

Who owns the next step?

Business leadership approves risk and exceptions. The MSP or internal IT team operates the tenant and implements approved changes. An independent reviewer can identify gaps, validate licensing constraints, prioritize fixes, and hand the plan back to the team already supporting the environment. Implementation and ongoing administration should be scoped separately.

Do not assume every Microsoft 365 subscription includes the same Conditional Access, Defender, or device-management features. Verify licenses and tenant configuration before prescribing controls.
