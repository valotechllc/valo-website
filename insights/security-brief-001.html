MSPs and clients: Stronger security through clear accountability

Great cybersecurity depends on teamwork: MSPs delivering day-to-day IT operations, specialists supporting complex controls, and business leaders making informed risk decisions.

Managed service providers (MSPs) are essential technology partners for many small and mid-sized organizations. They maintain networks, support users, manage platforms, and often implement critical security controls. Their knowledge of the client environment is a major advantage when security improvements are needed.

Effective cybersecurity also requires clear business decisions about risk. The goal is not to question an MSP’s value or duplicate its services; it is to make sure the MSP, the client, and any specialist security resources know which decisions belong to whom.

IT operations and security governance work together

IT operations are responsible for keeping systems available and usable: deploying devices, maintaining networks, administering Microsoft 365, handling backups, supporting users, patching systems, and resolving outages.

Cybersecurity governance asks a different set of questions. Which risks are acceptable? Who can approve an exception? Which controls are mandatory? How long can a high-risk finding remain open? Who reviews third-party risk? What evidence proves a control is operating? What does leadership need to know before accepting residual risk?

Some MSPs provide both technical operations and security advisory services; others collaborate with specialist providers. Either model can work. What matters is an agreed division of responsibilities, with the organization’s leadership accountable for accepting business risk and technical partners empowered to implement the agreed controls.

Clear ownership strengthens technical controls

A correctly configured firewall, MFA deployment, or backup program is more valuable when the client and its technical partners also agree on exceptions, testing, remediation priorities, and who approves residual risk. Good controls and clear accountability reinforce each other.

Five questions leadership should be able to answer

1. What are our top cybersecurity risks right now?

If the answer is a list of products—firewall, antivirus, MFA—that is not a risk register. Leadership should be able to identify the material scenarios that could disrupt the business, expose regulated data, create contractual problems, or cause significant financial loss.

2. Who owns each high-risk item?

A technical team may be responsible for implementing a control, but a business owner must still be accountable for the underlying risk. High-risk items should have named owners, target dates, treatment decisions, and escalation when work stalls.

3. Who can accept an exception?

Security exceptions happen. The problem is not that every environment has exceptions; the problem is when exceptions are informal, permanent, or invisible. Organizations need a defined process for documenting the reason, compensating controls, expiration date, and the person authorized to accept the remaining risk.

4. How do we know a control is actually working?

A policy document is not operating evidence. Neither is a checkbox in a questionnaire. Effective governance connects the written requirement to evidence: configuration, logs, access reviews, recovery tests, vendor reviews, tickets, attestations, or other artifacts that show the control exists in practice.

5. What does leadership review on a recurring basis?

Cybersecurity should not appear only during an audit, insurance renewal, customer questionnaire, or incident. Leadership needs a recurring view of top risks, remediation aging, exceptions, critical vendors, incidents, control health, and decisions that require executive ownership.

Put the MSP–client partnership to work

The strongest model usually builds on the relationship already in place. Business leaders define priorities and approve risk decisions. The MSP contributes environmental knowledge, runs day-to-day operations, and delivers agreed technical improvements. When a project calls for deeper expertise—for example, a Palo Alto firewall migration, Cisco network segmentation, or complex policy remediation—a specialist can support the MSP without disrupting the client relationship.

MSPs may also offer governance and security advisory services themselves. Where additional support is useful, responsibilities, reporting, client communications, and deliverables should be agreed up front. VALO’s MSP engagements are designed for scoped, white-label or subcontracted network security engineering, with client-relationship expectations set in the engagement agreement.

A practical starting point

Use the five questions above as a joint discussion agenda for the business and its MSP. Review the most material risks, confirm which controls and evidence already exist, identify any gaps, and agree on owners, remediation priorities, and review dates.

If a focused technical review is needed, define its scope and deliverables before beginning. A firewall policy assessment or network security review can give the MSP and client a practical plan to act on together, rather than automatically adding a new security product or replacing a trusted provider.
