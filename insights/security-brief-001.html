Issue 001 · September 30, 2026 · Governance & Risk

Your MSP manages your IT. Who owns your cybersecurity risk?

The team keeping technology running may be doing excellent work and still not be the function that owns enterprise cybersecurity risk.

For many small and mid-sized organizations, the answer to “Who handles cybersecurity?” is some version of: our MSP does, our IT manager handles it, or the same team that manages Microsoft 365 and the firewall takes care of security.

That answer may describe who operates the technology. It does not necessarily describe who owns the risk.

IT operations and security governance solve different problems

IT operations are responsible for keeping systems available and usable: deploying devices, maintaining networks, administering Microsoft 365, handling backups, supporting users, patching systems, and resolving outages.

Cybersecurity governance asks a different set of questions. Which risks are acceptable? Who can approve an exception? Which controls are mandatory? How long can a high-risk finding remain open? Who reviews third-party risk? What evidence proves a control is operating? What does leadership need to know before accepting residual risk?

The two functions should work together. But when nobody explicitly owns the governance layer, important security decisions become assumptions instead of decisions.

The ownership gap

A firewall can be correctly configured and the organization can still have unmanaged cyber risk. MFA can be enabled and important exceptions can still be undocumented. Backups can exist and recovery can still be untested. Security tools do not replace risk ownership.

Five questions leadership should be able to answer

1. What are our top cybersecurity risks right now?

If the answer is a list of products—firewall, antivirus, MFA—that is not a risk register. Leadership should be able to identify the material scenarios that could disrupt the business, expose regulated data, create contractual problems, or cause significant financial loss.

2. Who owns each high-risk item?

A technical team may be responsible for implementing a control, but a business owner must still be accountable for the underlying risk. High-risk items should have named owners, target dates, treatment decisions, and escalation when work stalls.

3. Who can accept an exception?

Security exceptions happen. The problem is not that every environment has exceptions; the problem is when exceptions are informal, permanent, or invisible. Organizations need a defined process for documenting the reason, compensating controls, expiration date, and the person authorized to accept the remaining risk.

4. How do we know a control is actually working?

A policy document is not operating evidence. Neither is a checkbox in a questionnaire. Effective governance connects the written requirement to evidence: configuration, logs, access reviews, recovery tests, vendor reviews, tickets, attestations, or other artifacts that show the control exists in practice.

5. What does leadership review on a recurring basis?

Cybersecurity should not appear only during an audit, insurance renewal, customer questionnaire, or incident. Leadership needs a recurring view of top risks, remediation aging, exceptions, critical vendors, incidents, control health, and decisions that require executive ownership.

Where an MSP fits

A strong MSP can be an important part of the solution. In many organizations, the MSP is the team best positioned to implement technical changes because it already understands the environment. The governance function should not automatically replace that relationship.

Instead, the organization needs clarity about roles. The MSP or internal IT team may administer systems and execute remediation. Security leadership defines risk priorities, control expectations, exceptions, evidence, and accountability. In some organizations that leadership is a full-time CISO. In smaller organizations it may be an executive sponsor supported by a fractional security leader or outside advisor.

A practical starting point

If leadership cannot confidently answer the five questions above, the first step usually is not buying another security product. It is establishing a baseline: understand the material risks, document the control and evidence gaps, assign ownership, and create a remediation roadmap.

That is why VALO starts many conversations with a defined assessment rather than an open-ended retainer. The assessment should stand on its own. Once the organization understands the risk, it can decide whether remediation belongs with its internal team, its MSP, VALO, or another qualified provider.
