Issue 004 · November 11, 2026 · Network & Security Engineering

Quantum-safe encryption now has deadlines. Do they apply to you?

Most networks have a firewall, a VPN and a certificate on the management page. Very few have a list of which encryption those things actually use, or a plan for replacing it.

Issue 003 looked at phishing-resistant MFA. This issue looks one layer down, at the encryption that protects remote access, site-to-site tunnels, websites and admin logins, and at the deadlines that were set for it in 2026.

First, the reassuring part. CISA says a quantum computer able to break today's public-key encryption does not yet exist. The concern is public-key algorithms such as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. These set up encrypted sessions and prove identity. NIST says symmetric encryption such as AES is less vulnerable, and NIST does not expect to replace it as part of this transition.

What changed in 2026

June 22: Executive Order 14412. It requires federal high-value assets and high-impact systems to use post-quantum cryptography for key establishment by December 31, 2030, and for digital signatures by December 31, 2031.

June 24: OMB memo M-26-15. It tells federal agencies how to carry out the migration, including building post-quantum support into the requirements for products they buy.

October: NIST IR 8547 finalized. NIST will deprecate quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallow it under NIST standards and guidelines after 2035. The replacement algorithms were published in August 2024 as FIPS 203, 204 and 205. NIST also intends to accommodate hybrid techniques, which combine a post-quantum algorithm with a classical one during the transition.

Does it apply to you?

These mandates are aimed at federal agencies, not private businesses or state and local government. The effect still reaches you in three ways:

  • The vendors that sell to government are building post-quantum support into their products.
  • The executive order directs a proposed federal contracting rule.
  • NIST describes IR 8547 as a blueprint for critical infrastructure owners and other organizations.

If you hold federal contracts or grants, watch the contracting rule closely.

Five checks for this quarter

1. Find where public-key encryption lives

Start with the obvious places:

  • site-to-site and remote-access VPNs
  • firewall and switch management certificates
  • SSL/TLS decryption
  • public websites and client portals
  • certificate-based Wi-Fi

You don't need a tool to begin. A spreadsheet with the system, what it protects and who owns it is enough.

2. Flag long-lived data

Some records must stay confidential for years: patient, student, legal, financial and government records. The connections that carry them deserve priority over traffic that is worthless in a week.

3. Match it to your refresh cycle

Note which firewalls, VPN concentrators and appliances will still be in service after 2030. The cheapest migration is the one folded into a refresh you were already planning.

4. Ask vendors for a written roadmap

Ask which products support ML-KEM (FIPS 203) or hybrid key exchange today, in which software versions, and what is planned for the hardware you own. Support varies by product, model and release, so get it in writing.

5. Keep evidence

A simple cryptography inventory with owners and target dates is the record that auditors, cyber insurers and federal customers are likely to ask for. It also turns a vague worry into a dated plan.

Who does what

Leadership decides:

  • which data must stay confidential the longest
  • whether post-quantum readiness becomes a purchasing requirement
  • when refresh budgets land

The MSP or IT team handles the hands-on work:

  • builds and maintains the inventory
  • tracks vendor support and software versions
  • tests and schedules changes

Vendors and resellers provide written roadmaps and supported versions, and answer the questions in check 4 before a renewal, not after.

Planning the change without breaking connectivity

  1. Inventory before changing anything. Every VPN tunnel has two ends, and both must support the same settings.
  2. Upgrade to vendor-supported software first. Post-quantum options generally arrive in newer releases.
  3. Test on a lab setup or a single low-risk tunnel before touching production.
  4. Change one peer pair at a time, in a maintenance window, with a written rollback step.
  5. Retire the oldest, weakest settings you find along the way.
  6. Record what changed, when, and who approved it.

A useful first step this week costs nothing. Ask your MSP or IT team: which of our systems rely on RSA or elliptic-curve encryption today, and which of them will still be running after 2030?

Sources: Executive Order 14412; OMB M-26-15; NIST IR 8547; NIST FIPS 203, 204 and 205; CISA Post-Quantum Cryptography Initiative. Federal requirements and vendor support can change, so check current guidance for your environment.
