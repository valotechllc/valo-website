Issue 005 · November 25, 2026 · Network & Security Engineering

Your firewall is the front door. Is it still supported?

Firewalls, VPN gateways and other internet-facing devices are the first thing an attacker touches. When one is past end of support, or exposes more than it should, it becomes the easiest way in.

Issue 004 looked at the encryption those devices use. This issue looks at the devices themselves: what sits at the edge of your network, whether the vendor still fixes it, and what it exposes to the internet.

Edge devices are an attractive target for a simple reason. They are reachable from anywhere, they often run with privileged access to the network behind them, and they rarely run endpoint security tools. An attacker who gets in at the edge can often move inward from there.

What happened in the last few weeks

October 28: Cisco ASA and FTD. Cisco disclosed CVE-2026-76418 in the Remote Access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Secure Firewall Threat Defense (FTD). An unauthenticated attacker can force the device to reload, taking the VPN down. Cisco reports it is being exploited, rates it 8.6 (CVSS 3.1), and says there is no workaround, only fixed releases and a temporary mitigation.

November 20: Cisco Expressway. CISA added CVE-2026-76466 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog. It affects clustered Expressway deployments and lets an unauthenticated attacker run commands as root. Reported attacks started at internet-facing Expressway Edge devices and moved to internal systems from there. The fix is release X15.5.20 or later.

Neither of these is a reason to single out one vendor. Exploited edge-device flaws turn up across the industry. The pattern is the point: the edge is where attackers start.

What CISA has asked for

On February 5, 2026, CISA issued Binding Operational Directive 26-02, Mitigating Risk from End-of-Support Edge Devices. It is binding only on federal civilian agencies, with these deadlines:

  • Immediately: update supported devices that run end-of-support software.
  • May 5, 2026: inventory end-of-support edge devices.
  • February 5, 2027: begin removing devices that are already past end of support.
  • August 5, 2027: finish removing them.
  • February 5, 2028: run continuous discovery and lifecycle management.

In a joint fact sheet, CISA, the FBI and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre "strongly urge all organizations" to review the directive and apply the same mitigations. For businesses, MSPs and state and local government it is guidance, not a mandate, and it is a sensible bar to measure against.

Five checks for this quarter

1. List every edge device

Include anything that faces the internet or routes traffic between networks:

  • firewalls and VPN gateways
  • routers and internet-facing switches
  • wireless controllers and access points
  • load balancers
  • voice and video gateways, such as Expressway

For each one, record the model, software version, owner and location. A spreadsheet is enough to start.

2. Add the end-of-support date

Look up each model and software train on the vendor's end-of-life notices. Flag anything already past end of support, and anything that reaches it in the next 12 months. Those devices will not get fixes for the next exploited flaw.

3. Patch what is still supported

Use CISA's KEV catalog to decide what goes first: a flaw that is being exploited outranks a higher score that is not. Check whether your ASA, FTD or Expressway releases are on the fixed lists in Cisco's advisories.

4. Shrink what the internet can reach

Management interfaces should not be reachable from the internet. VPN portals should be on only where they are used. Cluster and peer ports should accept traffic only from known peers. Cisco's Expressway fix guidance, for example, limits TCP ports 4371–4372 to cluster nodes when you can't update right away.

5. Keep evidence

Keep the inventory with owners, end-of-support dates, patch dates and replacement plans. Auditors, cyber insurers and public-sector customers increasingly ask how edge devices are managed. It also gives leadership a dated list to budget against.

Who does what

Leadership decides:

  • how much end-of-support risk the organization will accept, and for how long
  • when replacement budgets land
  • who signs off on any exception

The MSP or IT team handles the hands-on work:

  • builds and maintains the inventory
  • watches vendor advisories and the KEV catalog
  • patches, hardens and schedules replacements

Vendors and resellers provide end-of-life dates, supported upgrade paths and replacement options, in writing.

Replacing an edge device without an outage

  1. Export the running configuration and store it off the device.
  2. Translate it to the new platform and review every rule, NAT and VPN entry, instead of copying blindly.
  3. Test with a written list built by the people who use the network: remote VPN, payroll, phones, door controllers.
  4. Cut over in a maintenance window, with a rollback deadline agreed in advance and out-of-band access ready.
  5. Keep the old device racked and untouched until the new one has carried a full business day.
  6. Record what changed, when, and who approved it.

Before the holiday weekend

Fewer people are watching over Thanksgiving. Before you log off, confirm three things: internet-facing devices are on fixed releases for the advisories above, someone is on call who can reach them out of band, and that person knows who to call at the vendor or MSP.

A useful first step costs nothing. Ask your MSP or IT team: which of our internet-facing devices are past end of support, or will be in the next 12 months?

Sources: CISA BOD 26-02; CISA, FBI and NCSC: Reducing the Attack Surface for End-of-Support Edge Devices; Cisco advisory: ASA and FTD SSL VPN (CVE-2026-76418); Cisco advisory: Expressway (CVE-2026-76466); CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Fixed releases and guidance can change, so check the current advisories for your environment.
